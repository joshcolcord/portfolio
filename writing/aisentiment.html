Where Do We Go From Here?

Josh Colcord - Nov 3, 2026

This post has been a long time coming. I’ve spent many nights considering, doubting, sometimes fearing the coming state of programming, but hopefully my perspective can shed some light, maybe even some hope, on what the future might bring.

Firstly, two facts I'm considering:

  • Those who write code by hand cannot type faster than an LLM can.
  • The value of raw code is now very, very cheap.

I honestly don’t think either of these can be seriously disputed. Consider the Vim warriors of the past (every keybind memorized.. you know who you are) who, despite being blazing, cannot reach the throughput of 200 tokens-per-second and onwards that we see nowadays (if you can, please contact me). Then also, per OpenRouter’s finest analytics, GLM 5.3 Flash provides amazing performance at the expensive cost of.. ~8 cents per million tokens. Wonderful.

It seems a very dire situation for the average software engineer, then, if writing code doesn’t hold value. What is my value then? Well.. it’s a great question to ask.

Elegy of the Draftsman

A comparison I have recently been considering is to engineers from times past; blueprinting and sketching with pens and pencils. This job specifically used to be that of a “draftsman” (give this article a read). In brief, the career of draftsmen used to be lucrative and highly in-demand. Engineering powerhouses such as Lockheed Martin and the like kept armadas of draftsmen and draftswomen whose jobs were solely to translate the wills of engineers into actionable blueprints and schematics. Without drafting, no tangible result would come from engineering.

We no longer have draftsmen.

As computer-aided design (CAD) matured, the role was no longer needed. The computer could translate the whims of the engineer as swiftly, or even swifter, than an experienced drafter could, and an engineer could bring an idea from the inner workings of their mind to a fully actionable schematic in.. maybe a day? A few hours? You can have no doubt of the fear in the drafters’ hearts at this rise in technology, especially considering the many who very unfortunately found themselves dismissed.

I feel comfortable talking on this subject because I worked heavily with CAD software, in my case Autodesk Fusion 360 and PTC Onshape, during my time in competitive robotics, doing model creation, FEA optimization, and much more. Not once did I contact a draftsman for my work.

The story of the draftsman, although a sad one, closely mirrors what we see today. Code is exactly the form in which we interact with the machine. Decades went by with elegant solutions being pored over for hours and succinctly implemented. Those days are not over, nor will they ever be, but they’re shrinking to minutes. In a sense, code is drafts and blueprints: simply a medium that lays out exactly what must be done. The value shifts from picking up the pencil to understanding what it drew, acting on it, building systems with it, true engineering.

Writing for the Mind

In a way, LLM coding is simply the latest evolution of information technology. In his dialogue Phaedrus, Plato critiques writing (writing!) because it reduces the active thought necessary to form and store information. Of course it seems silly to us now, since writing is ubiquitous, but in that day writing was the next form of information.

I agree with both sides of his argument: it does reduce the active thought necessary to process and act on information, but it also frees up the mental overhead needed to tackle that information, letting any individual handle far more knowledge than in years past. There’s a quote which loosely states “AI is both the best and the worst thing to ever happen to learning;” almost the same thing. A fool may ignore learning in favor of relying upon scrolls to store his or her knowledge. The wise individual will use both their scrolls and their own mind to learn and create more than ever before.

A quick shout to Two Sigma’s 2026 intern class; per Business Insider, they completed far more than any previous summer, and that’s not because the interns were magically smarter; it’s because they were able to use both their minds and knowledge-on-tap to complete just that much more.

System of Code

One of the main reasons I believe the industry is going through such a tumultuous time right now is the growing pains and friction of engineers who have written code the standard, artisanal, draft-like way for decades. I mean absolutely no disrespect; I have the highest regard for those who hold such skills and who have had the means and tenure to apply them for as long as they have.

But, in this new generation, the focus shifts far from handwritten code to a system-of-code. Like the engineers who handed their sketches to draftsmen, engineers today focus on the overall design of an engine, not the sketches that form a single piston. Unfortunately, junior engineers took the role of draftsmen for all previous years of software engineering, and I’m not quite sure where they (I) go from here, but it is also important to recognize the value in investing in young talent and growing them into the future seniors of your company.

My honest, singular approach to using LLMs in my work has been to check the output of all code written and make sure it matches exactly what I would be writing as a developer anyways; vibe coding is indeed inherently unwise. However, LLMs can write faster than humanly possible and that’s a great advantage for the concious developer writing code.

In this way, I think the future of the software engineer lies in leveraging LLMs to write code while using them to understand the code being written, and the effects and consequences of each function within that. The largest shock that LLM-based code brings is that the ability to simply write code was perhaps never actually the core value of a software engineer. It was the tool used. But, in the days of past, the pencil was never the engineer. Now, neither is the keyboard.
